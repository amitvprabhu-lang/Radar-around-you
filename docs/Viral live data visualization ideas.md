# Planes, quakes and starlight top the shortlist

The best idea for you is a live "how many planes are in the sky right now" globe, paired with **Mid-Air Strangers**: a tool that replays a past flight and shows every airliner you crossed at cruising altitude. It matches the exact live-count question that brings Orbital Radar most of its search traffic. No live page answers that question on Google's first page today. Its main data source (adsb.lol) is licensed under ODbL, which allows an ad-supported site. And the personal "strangers you passed" result doesn't seem to exist anywhere yet. Next come **Seismic Ripple** (watch a quake's waves cross the planet and see the second they passed under your city) and **Starlight Time Machine** (tap a star overhead to see what was happening on Earth when its light left). After those come **Lost Stars** (the stars your sky has lost since you were born), **Fireball Window** and **Packet Passport**. I weighted viral pull and search pull equally, then broke ties on licence cleanliness and on how much one developer can realistically build. Orbital Radar's lesson is that the globe gets the links, but simple live-count answer pages get the traffic. One "live count" page earns about 64,000 of its roughly 210,000 estimated monthly US search visits, according to a third-party Ubersuggest estimate. Search demand for every idea here is inferred from Google Autocomplete and observed rankings, not measured volumes, so check each niche in a keyword tool before you commit. **No idea here is guaranteed to go viral.** In the precedents studied, virality mostly came from a news event or a lucky share that nobody could plan, so each pick is designed to earn steady search traffic even if that moment never comes.

## Orbital Radar wins on answer pages, not on its globe

The Ubersuggest export you supplied covers the US English database and search traffic only, so it shows nothing about social, direct or non-US visits, and all its figures are third-party estimates. It credits orbitalradar.com with about **209,600 monthly organic visits** from about **42,500 ranking keywords**, and no paid traffic. A single page, "How Many Satellites Are in Orbit? Live Count 2026", earns about **64,000 of those visits (about 31 percent) with only 63 backlinks**. It ranks first for "how many satellites are circling the earth" (about 8,100 monthly searches) and for four near-identical phrasings at about 6,600 each. The homepage holds about 7,300 of the domain's 8,100 backlinks but earns only about 1,700 search visits. Templated pages such as "Satellites of China, Live Count & Programmes 2026" earn about 1,700 visits each from a single backlink, and the site ranks first for "satellite russia". The five pages Ubersuggest names add up to only about 72,000 visits, so roughly two thirds of the estimated traffic comes from a long tail of other pages (assuming Ubersuggest's page and domain estimates add up). That tail is still climbing: in September only about 940 keywords ranked in positions 1 to 3, against about 24,700 in positions 11 to 50 (Ubersuggest export supplied by the user, 4 Oct 2026). The site calls itself "independent & self-funded", says donations cover "servers, data licensing and development", offers enterprise data licensing and publishes dated news briefings ([Orbital Radar mission page](https://orbitalradar.com/our-mission); [Orbital Radar briefing, 2 Oct 2026](https://orbitalradar.com/blog/orbital-briefing-2026-10-02)).

The timeline is short. The domain was registered on **5 February 2025** through GoDaddy ([Verisign RDAP](https://rdap.verisign.com/com/v1/domain/orbitalradar.com)). You say the site was built in 2026; that could not be checked because the Wayback Machine was unreachable. Search traffic was close to zero until about May 2026. Reading the chart, it then grew to roughly 35,000 visits in July, 130,000 in August and 210,000 in September (approximate). Domain authority rose from about 9 in April to about 43 in September on the chart, although the headline figure says 39. Backlinks stayed in the low hundreds until July, then jumped to about 3,200 in August and 8,100 in September. Because traffic started climbing before the backlink jump, the question pages were already ranking before the links arrived. The jump probably sped up August and September, but these estimates cannot prove cause and effect. The site offers an embeddable widget, and widget embeds would explain why the links point mostly at the homepage, but that is an unverified guess.

Three lessons shape the ranking below. First, the winning search is "how many X are there right now". Its answer keeps changing, so a static article goes stale and an AI summary struggles to state it precisely. Second, the 3D globe earns links and shares, while plain, fast answer pages earn the search visits, so every idea needs both. Third, Orbital Radar entered a crowded field. KeepTrack (about 1,600 GitHub stars) and satellitemap.space already existed, as did Stuff in Space, whose 2015 Hacker News launch scored 629 points ([KeepTrack GitHub](https://github.com/thkruz/keeptrack.space); [HN Algolia: Stuff in Space](https://hn.algolia.com/api/v1/search?query=stuff%20in%20space&tags=story&hitsPerPage=10)). Turning live data into answers to people's questions mattered more than being first. It also built on clean data: Space-Track gives blanket approval to redistribute orbital elements, the satellite catalogue and re-entry data as long as you cite it ([Space-Track documentation](https://www.space-track.org/documentation)), which leaves the door open for ads.

## Six ideas ranked on viral pull and search pull

The 1 to 5 scores are my judgement, based on the evidence in the research notes. **Viral pull** rewards four traits that recur in the precedents (Wind Map, Stuff in Space, Radio Garden and NASA's Hubble birthday page): a visual you understand in one second, a personal result worth sharing, a clear gap among existing tools, and news events that keep bringing people back. **Search pull** rewards Autocomplete demand with "right now", "tonight" or "near me" variants, the lack of a live answer on page one, and a natural page template where each page carries different data. ### 1. Mid-Air Strangers: the airliners you passed at 35,000 feet

**Concept and hook.** One globe with two front doors. The search door is a live page that answers "how many planes are in the sky right now". It shows every airliner the network can see as a glowing point drifting over a night-side Earth, with a ticking count. The viral door is Mid-Air Strangers. You enter a flight number and date, and the globe replays your flight in about ten seconds. Every other commercial aircraft that crossed your path lights up, and the closest pass gets its own callout: horizontal gap, vertical separation, closing speed and where that plane was heading. The share card writes itself, for example "On my Delhi to Mumbai flight I passed 38 airliners, the closest 6 km away" (illustrative numbers). The competitor check found no automatic version of this. Flightradar24 lets you scrub its Global Playback by hand, with 7 days of free history, and a user on its feature-request thread asked for nearby aircraft to appear in 3D playback, which suggests the feature is missing ([FR24 support](https://support.fr24.com/support/solutions/articles/3000115499-is-it-possible-to-see-playback-of-past-flights-); [FR24 feature request thread](https://www.flightradar24.com/blog/threads/what-feature-would-you-like-to-see-added-to-flightradar24/)). Bellingcat's Turnstone (March 2026) can query historical ADS-B data, but it is built for investigators, not passengers ([Bellingcat Turnstone](https://www.bellingcat.com/resources/2026/03/05/turnstone-flight-tracking-tool/)). Flight tracking has proven spikes of attention: about 708,000 people tracked one flight at the same moment during Pelosi's 2022 Taiwan trip ([AirlineRatings](https://www.airlineratings.com/articles/pelosis-taiwan-flight-was-the-most-followed-on-fr24)).

**Live element and data.** One server job polls adsb.lol's live API (it has point-and-radius endpoints) every minute or so for the counts. adsb.lol also publishes daily history releases on GitHub, with up to at least 2 October 2026 available, and these feed the encounter engine ([adsb.lol OpenAPI](https://api.adsb.lol/api/openapi.json); [adsb.lol historical data](https://www.adsb.lol/docs/open-data/historical/); [globe_history_2026 release](https://github.com/adsblol/globe_history_2026/releases/tag/v2026.10.02-planes-readsb-prod-0)).

| Source | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| adsb.lol live API and daily history | Clean | ODbL 1.0 allows commercial use with attribution, but anything you publish as a database must be shared under the same licence. The owner asks production users to get in touch, and an API key earned by feeding data is planned |
| OpenSky Network | Restricted | Licensed "solely for the purpose of non-profit research and non-profit education"; any live product needs a written agreement ([OpenSky terms](https://opensky-network.org/about/terms-of-use)) |
| airplanes.live | Restricted | API now limited to feeders or approved projects ([airplanes.live API guide](https://airplanes.live/api-guide/)) |

**Search demand and templated pages.** Autocomplete shows "how many planes are in the sky right now", "...in the air right now", "how many flights are in the air right now", "how many people are in the air right now" and "how close do planes pass each other" ([Autocomplete: how many planes are](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=how%20many%20planes%20are); [Autocomplete: how close do planes](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=how%20close%20do%20planes)). The observed page one for the first query is all static articles. They quote ranges of 8,000 to 20,000 (Flightradar24) or 12,000 to 14,000 (FlightAware) and send readers off to the trackers ([Reader's Digest](https://www.rd.com/article/how-many-planes-are-in-the-sky/); [AeroCorner](https://aerocorner.com/blog/how-many-planes-in-the-sky-right-now/)). That is the clearest Orbital Radar-style gap the research found. A viral TikTok already pushes "how close do planes pass" searches, and TheStreet ranks on the back of it ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/travel/pilot-video-planes-flying-close-tiktok)). The research could not see whether Google shows its own flight modules for these queries.

The page template has four tiers. The first is one hero page: a live counter, a 24-hour curve and a split by continent. The second is "Planes over [country] right now" pages, the equivalent of "Satellites of China", indexed only for countries where a coverage meter shows real receiver density. The third is "Sky above [airport]: aircraft within 100 km now" for a few hundred busy airports. The fourth is route statistics pages such as "How many planes do you pass flying Delhi to Mumbai?", showing the median number of encounters and the closest pass over the last 30 days. That is original data nobody else publishes. Keep individual users' results shareable but noindex, so they never pile up as thousands of near-identical pages.

**Risks.** Coverage is the big one. An analysis of one day of adsb.lol data in November 2023 found India's clusters "nowhere near representative" and almost no aircraft in the Gulf outside Dubai. Ground receivers also cannot see the open ocean beyond roughly 250 nautical miles ([Mark Litwintschik](https://tech.marksblogg.com/global-flight-tracking-adsb.html)). That data is almost three years old, but it means Indian users will see thinner results, so grey out uncovered stretches and say so. Label the count "aircraft seen by adsb.lol receivers" so it never pretends to be a census. You would depend on one volunteer network. That kind of source can change hands, as ADS-B Exchange did when it was sold in 2023 ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2023/01/26/ads-b-exchange-sells-up-contributors-unhappy/)), so contact adsb.lol early and consider running a feeder. For privacy, show airline callsigns only, drop LADD and PIA aircraft (privacy-protected registrations) and never show private jets: jet-tracking accounts were banned by X in 2022 and Meta in 2024 ([Wikipedia: ElonJet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ElonJet)). Frame the "closest pass" as normal separation so it doesn't frighten nervous flyers. And Flightradar24 could ship the feature itself.

**Feasibility.** The live count is weekend-scale work: one scheduled job, a static JSON file and a globe. The encounter engine is the hardest data work on this list. A nightly job has to ingest the day's per-aircraft files (the 2023 analysis counted 38 million traces in one day), filter them to airline callsigns, build a time-and-space index and save the results as static files. One developer who is comfortable with batch data processing can do it. Ship the count page first as a cheap SEO test.

### 2. Seismic Ripple: the second a quake's waves passed under you

**Concept and hook.** When a significant quake hits, the globe animates its P and S waves (the faster first waves and the slower shaking waves that follow) spreading from the epicentre as glowing rings across the surface, and through the interior in a cutaway view. Enter your city and you get a personal timestamp: the waves from that quake passed under your home at 12:07:33 and moved the ground by a few micrometres. Live counters for "earthquakes today" and "earthquakes in 2026 so far" sit around this. A later twist is to show the real wiggle from the nearest public seismometer at the predicted moment, turned into sound, but the licence terms below make that an add-on, not the core.

**Live element and data.** USGS GeoJSON feeds update every minute with no key, and the EMSC (European-Mediterranean Seismological Centre) websocket pushes new events as they happen ([USGS feeds](https://earthquake.usgs.gov/earthquakes/feed/v1.0/geojson.php); [EMSC real-time](https://www.seismicportal.eu/realtime.html)).

| Source | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| USGS earthquake feeds | Clean | US public domain, credit requested ([USGS copyrights](https://www.usgs.gov/information-policies-and-instructions/copyrights-and-credits)) |
| EMSC websocket | Clean | Data "distributed under the CC BY 4.0 license" |
| EarthScope/IRIS waveforms | Treat as restricted until checked | Licence not checked; the usage policy bans polling its web services for real-time data and caps you at 5 concurrent connections ([IRIS DMC usage](https://ds.iris.edu/ds/nodes/dmc/services/usage/)) |
| Raspberry Shake | Restricted | "Strictly prohibited to redistribute real-time data", including for paying customers ([Raspberry Shake licence](https://raspberryshake.org/license/)) |

**Competitors and difference.** GlobalQuake animates live P and S waves and offers experimental alerts, but it is a desktop app plus a livestream aimed at enthusiasts ([GlobalQuake](https://globalquake.net/)). Princeton's ShakeMovie publishes simulations for M5+ quakes 90 to 180 minutes after the event ([ShakeMovie Global](https://global.shakemovie.princeton.edu/science.jsp)). IRIS's Seismic Waves Viewer covers only historic quakes and has no "your location" option ([IRIS viewer](https://www.iris.edu/hq/inclass/software-web-app/seismic_waves_viewer)). Gelogia's calculator gives arrival times as plain numbers ([Gelogia](https://gelogia.com/seismic-wave-arrival-calculator/)). None of them offers a one-tap, phone-friendly "it passed under you" moment with a share card.

**Search demand and templated pages.** This niche has the richest live-question demand in the research. Autocomplete offers "how many earthquakes today", "...in 2026 so far", "...in the last 30 days" and "is there an earthquake right now", plus city variants ([Autocomplete: how many earthquakes](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=how%20many%20earthquakes); [Autocomplete: is there an earthquake](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=is%20there%20an%20earthquake)). It is also crowded. Small USGS-based tools hold page one for "earthquake right now near me live map", including earthquaketrack.com with its templated place pages ([earthquaketracker.org](https://www.earthquaketracker.org/earthquakes/near-me); [earthquaketrack.com](https://earthquaketrack.com/p/united-states/california/recent)). That proves small sites can rank, but another plain map will not stand out. "How fast do seismic waves travel" returns only educational pages ([PNSN](https://pnsn.org/education/seismology/earthquake-waves)). The plan has three parts. First, a page for each M5+ or felt quake, with the wave animation, an arrival-time table for major cities and a link to felt reports; this is the citable URL journalists need. Second, "Earthquakes in [country] today and this year" pages with live counts and charts, mirroring Orbital Radar's country template. Third, a seismic-speed explainer that uses today's biggest quake as a live worked example, so it isn't a generic article. Don't build a page for every city and quake combination; put a city lookup on each quake's page instead. Noindex pages for small remote quakes that nobody felt.

**Risks and feasibility.** Quakes kill people, so the tone has to be respectful, and the tool must never look like a warning system. GlobalQuake labels its own alerts experimental. Simple constant-speed rings are inaccurate, so compute arrivals from precomputed travel-time tables and label them estimates. USGS catalogues small quakes most completely in the US. Outside it, USGS M4.5+ plus EMSC is a reasonable global layer and covers India. Traffic will be spiky, peaking with news. The build is medium effort: the feed is trivial, the waves are shader work, and the travel-time model needs one-off research. There is no per-visitor backend.

### 3. Starlight Time Machine: what Earth was doing when that light left

**Concept and hook.** Open the page at night and it draws the real sky above your location. Tap a star and a beam of light travels from it to Earth while a timeline rewinds to the year that light set off, with what was happening on Earth then. Most visitors can be given a "birth-year star": the star overhead tonight whose light left the year they were born. You can switch between history lenses: world, India, cricket, music, science. A reverse "radio bubble" mode shows the sphere of broadcasts spreading out from Earth. It names the stars that broadcasts from your birth year have already passed, and counts down to the next one. The competitor check found no tool that combines the live local sky, real distances to each star, history events and a share card. What exists is a static PBS table last updated in 2008, plus magazine pieces such as Space.com's note that Spica's light left around 1776 ([PBS Birthday Stars](https://www.pbs.org/seeinginthedark/explore-the-sky/birthday-stars.html); [Space.com on Spica](https://www.space.com/stargazing/this-stars-light-has-been-crossing-space-since-the-american-revolution-heres-how-to-find-it); [Sky at Night](https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/space-science/space-night-sky-look-back-in-time)). On the radio side, Cosmic Odometer tells you where an observer would need to be to see your birth, but doesn't name the stars your signals have reached ([Cosmic Odometer](https://www.cosmicodometer.space/)). Custom star maps show there's a market for "my sky": The Night Sky says it has created over a million, a self-reported figure ([The Night Sky](https://www.thenightsky.com/)).

**Live element and data.** This is the least live idea on the list. Star positions are computed in real time for your place and minute, and tonight's Moon and planets come from an on-device calculation or the free USNO API ([USNO API](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/data/api)). A naked-eye sky needs only stars down to about magnitude 6.5, which keeps the catalogue small.

|---|---|---|

| ESA Gaia, Hipparcos and Tycho | Restricted | Now CC BY-NC 3.0 IGO, and ESA's archive terms demand a licence request before any use that "directly or indirectly generates a financial gain" ([ESDC terms](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/esdc/terms-and-conditions); [Hipparcos catalogues](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/hipparcos/catalogues); [Gaia licence](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/gaia-users/license)) |

| Stellarium Web Engine (code) | Clean, with conditions | AGPL-3.0 means you must open-source a modified hosted version, and its Gaia star access brings in ESA's terms ([Stellarium Web Engine](https://github.com/Stellarium/stellarium-web-engine)) |
| Wikipedia text for events | Clean | CC BY-SA, so attribution and share-alike ([Wikitech EventStreams](https://wikitech.wikimedia.org/wiki/Event_Platform/EventStreams_HTTP_Service)) |

On your HYG question: yes, HYG is the most practical licence-friendly alternative to Gaia and Hipparcos. CC BY-SA 4.0 permits commercial use, and the catalogue easily covers the naked-eye sky. The catch is that HYG redistributes Hipparcos-derived values under CC BY-SA while ESA now labels Hipparcos CC BY-NC. The notes could not find when ESA switched; earlier Gaia releases were widely described as CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO, so this looks like a recent change. Before you add ads, email the HYG maintainer and ask whether the Hipparcos-derived fields are cleared for commercial reuse. If the answer is unclear, ask ESA for a licence covering the small naked-eye subset.

**Search demand and templated pages.** This is the weakest search case of the top three. "How long does starlight" autocompletes entirely to a parade, a video game and Starlink ([Autocomplete: how long does starlight](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=how%20long%20does%20starlight)), and "what star was" drifts toward celebrities and zodiac signs ([Autocomplete: what star was](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=what%20star%20was)). Nearby live sky questions are strong, though: "what star is in the western sky tonight", "what star is next to the moon tonight" and "what was the night sky when i was born" ([Autocomplete: what star is](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=what%20star%20is); [Autocomplete: what was the night sky](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=what%20was%20the%20night%20sky)). "How far have radio signals traveled from earth" also shows up ([Autocomplete: how far have radio](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=how%20far%20have%20radio)). A small per-star site, star-facts.com, ranks for "how far away is Betelgeuse", which shows per-star templates can work ([star-facts.com](https://www.star-facts.com/betelgeuse/)). The plan is a page per named naked-eye star, with its distance shown with uncertainty, the range of years its light left, a few curated events and where to find it tonight. Add "Stars whose light left in [year]" pages only for years that have enough well-measured stars, and one location-aware "What's that bright star tonight?" tool instead of hundreds of city clones.

**Risks and feasibility.** Distances to far-off stars carry wide error bars, so show year ranges rather than false precision. Be honest about radio. EarthSky notes our leakage is far too faint to be intelligible even at Proxima Centauri ([EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/space/earths-radio-bubble-extent-of-radio-signals/)), so say "the wavefront has passed", never "they can hear you". It's a one-off novelty, so add tonight features to bring people back. Static distance facts are exactly what AI summaries answer without a click. The upside is that this is the cheapest build on the list. The catalogue is a few megabytes, every calculation runs on the device, and hosting costs are near zero. The real work is curating accurate, well-dated events and building a beautiful sky renderer, which Lost Stars can then reuse.

### 4. Lost Stars: the sky you had when you were born

**Concept and hook.** "How many stars can you see from home tonight, and how many has your sky lost since you were born?" The sky dome dims year by year from your birth year to now, while a glow rises from the city horizon. The card names a casualty, such as "since 1995 your sky lost the Little Dipper". Satellite night-light records start in 2012. For earlier years, use the global average from the Globe at Night analysis, which found sky brightness rising about **9.6 percent a year** from 2011 to 2022, and label those years as modelled ([NOIRLab](https://kpno.noirlab.edu/news/noirlab2302/)). The live layer comes from tonight's Moon and cloud cover, because the light-pollution data itself updates monthly or yearly.

| Source | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| NASA Black Marble (VIIRS VNP46) | Clean | NASA open data; lightpollutionmap.info points users to the yearly product "under CC0". An Earthdata login may be needed, which is unconfirmed ([lightpollutionmap.info help](https://www.lightpollutionmap.info/help.html); [LAADS VNP46A1](https://ladsweb.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/missions-and-measurements/products/VNP46A1/)) |
| EOG VIIRS Nighttime Lights | Clean for most products | "Many" products are CC BY 4.0; check each one ([EOG VNL](https://eogdata.mines.edu/products/vnl/)) |
| World Atlas of Artificial Night Sky Brightness | Grey area | CC BY-NC 4.0 since November 2019 ([GFZ World Atlas](https://dataservices.gfz-potsdam.de/contact/showshort.php?id=escidoc:1541893)) |
| lightpollutionmap.info tiles | Treat as restricted | Credit required and no published licence for reusing tiles; link to it rather than embedding ([lightpollutionmap.info](https://www.lightpollutionmap.info/)) |
| NOAA GFS and ECMWF clouds | Clean | NOAA open data "can be used as desired"; ECMWF has been CC BY 4.0 since October 2025 ([AWS: NOAA GFS](https://registry.opendata.aws/noaa-gfs-bdp-pds/); [ECMWF open data](https://www.ecmwf.int/en/forecasts/datasets/open-data)) |
| HYG stars | Clean on its face | As in idea 3 |

**Competitors and difference.** This is a crowded field. lightpollutionmap.app already shows visible stars, Bortle class (the 1 to 9 darkness scale) and an interactive sky view, with VIIRS layers from 2012 to 2025 ([lightpollutionmap.app](https://lightpollutionmap.app/)). Academo slides between city and pristine skies, and calculators estimate star counts ([Academo Bortle Scale](https://academo.org/demos/bortle-scale/); [Star Visibility Calculator](https://miniwebtool.com/star-visibility-calculator/); [Dark Site Finder](https://darksitefinder.com/)). The new parts are the "since you were born" time baseline, the emotional fade animation, and depth for India, where VIIRS coverage is as complete as anywhere.

**Search demand and templated pages.** "Light pollution map" autocompletes to "...2026", "...usa" and a string of state names, which is a ready-made template ([Autocomplete: light pollution map](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=light%20pollution%20map)). "How many stars are in the sky" adds "tonight" and "right now" ([Autocomplete](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=how%20many%20stars%20are%20in%20the%20sky)), and the "naked eye" version is answered by static articles ([Love the Night Sky](https://lovethenightsky.com/how-many-stars-can-i-see/)). Build pages per US state, per Indian state and district, and per major world city. Each one gets an estimated Bortle class, estimated visible stars, VIIRS brightening since 2012, the nearest darker site and tonight's conditions, so every page carries its own numbers. Add a yearly "night sky brightening" ranking for journalists to cite. Noindex small places whose numbers barely differ from their neighbours'.

**Risks and feasibility.** Converting satellite radiance into sky brightness is an approximation; the World Atlas does it better but is non-commercial. The 9.6 percent figure is a global average from naked-eye observations, not a measurement for each city, so label it as an estimate. Medium effort: a one-time raster job turns the annual VIIRS composites into a per-place lookup table, and everything after that is static and reuses the idea 3 sky engine.

### 5. Fireball Window: the nearest fireball to your home, replayed from your window

**Concept and hook.** One 3D globe shows every sensor-recorded fireball since 1988, plus last night's camera-network meteors with their orbits traced back into the solar system. It adds tonight's meteor shower forecast for your city. Your personal result is "the closest recorded fireball to your home was X km away in YEAR". A "From your window" replay then shows how that fireball would have looked from your own location: its direction, height in the sky and brightness. Big fireballs are recurring news: the American Meteor Society logged 2,046 events in the first quarter of 2026, including 38 with 50 or more witness reports, against a quarterly average of 18 ([EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/space/flurry-of-fireballs-march-2026-reason-ams/)). National outlets cover multi-state fireballs ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/fireball-meteorite-georgia-south-carolina/)).

| Source | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Global Meteor Network | Clean | CC BY 4.0, updated every 6 hours, with a SQL/REST interface ([GMN data](https://globalmeteornetwork.org/data/)) |
| JPL CNEOS fireball and close-approach APIs | Clean data, strict access | US government sensor data, but JPL's fair-use policy says "you may not embed these APIs in your website", so cache them on your own server. They are not real time: on 4 Oct 2026 the newest record was dated 15 Sep 2026 ([JPL SSD API](https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/); [Fireball API](https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/doc/fireball.html); [CAD API](https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/doc/cad.html)) |
| NOAA GOES lightning mapper (detects bright meteors) | Clean | NOAA open data, Americas only ([AWS: NOAA GOES](https://registry.opendata.aws/noaa-goes/); [GOES-R GLM](https://www.goes-r.gov/spacesegment/glm.html)) |
| American Meteor Society | Unknown; treat as restricted | Much of the site has been offline since a cyberattack, so its terms couldn't be read ([AMS notice](https://www.amsmeteors.org/ams-programs/fireball-api/); [The Record](https://therecord.media/international-meteor-organization-cyberattack)) |
| Space-Track re-entry data (optional) | Clean with citation | Covered by the blanket redistribution approval, but Orbital Radar already covers re-entries |

**Competitors and difference.** Asteroid Alerts already runs a live fireball log with free push alerts ([Asteroid Alerts](https://asteroidalerts.com/fireballs)). Several 2D maps exist: CNEOS, Live Earth Reports, and LiveEarthViewer, which shows meteor shower peak nights by city ([CNEOS](https://cneos.jpl.nasa.gov/fireballs/); [Live Earth Reports](https://www.liveearthreports.com/fireballs-meteors-reports/); [LiveEarthViewer](https://liveearthviewer.com/sky/meteor-showers)). The NASA/SETI shower portal shows orbits but not individual fireballs ([meteorshowers.org](https://www.meteorshowers.org/)). What's new here is merging all the datasets on one 3D globe, the personal "nearest to you" result, and the replay from your own viewpoint.

**Search demand and templated pages.** "Meteor shower tonight" expands to "near me", "what time", "where to look", "live" and state names ([Autocomplete](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=meteor%20shower%20tonight)). "Did a meteor" turns into local news searches such as "did a meteor hit ohio" ([Autocomplete](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=did%20a%20meteor)). Bare "fireball" is useless because it means whisky and a D&D spell ([Autocomplete](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=fireball)). Small tools such as meteorshower.app already rank next to Space.com and EarthSky ([meteorshower.app](https://meteorshower.app/en/tonight)). Build a page per major shower, with a live countdown, how high the radiant (the point meteors appear to come from) rises at each latitude, and moonlight interference. Allow shower-and-city pages only for a capped set of cities spread across latitudes, and give each notable fireball its own event page for journalists to link to.

**Risks and feasibility.** The data is fragmented and slow (CNEOS lags by weeks), and the best eyewitness source, AMS, is partly offline. Eyewitness data clusters in North America and Europe, so pages about India will be thin. Traffic is seasonal and spiky. Medium effort: everything runs as scheduled server jobs writing static JSON, and the "from your window" geometry is the one tricky piece.

### 6. Packet Passport: where your click really goes

**Concept and hook.** Pick a popular website. A glowing packet travels from your city through landing stations and undersea cables to a data centre, and you get a stamped "passport" listing the countries and cables it likely crossed, the kilometres it travelled, and how close your measured round-trip time came to the speed of light. A 3D cable globe has proven appeal: a third-party globe.gl rendering of cable data scored 686 points on Hacker News, more than any post of the official flat map ([HN Algolia: submarine cable map](https://hn.algolia.com/api/v1/search?query=submarine%20cable%20map&tags=story&hitsPerPage=8)).

| Source | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| TeleGeography Submarine Cable Map data | Grey area until confirmed | The old GitHub data says CC BY-NC-SA 3.0; the current citation page says CC BY-SA 4.0, possibly for the map images only. The JSON endpoints are undocumented ([TeleGeography citation](https://www2.telegeography.com/cite-telegeography-map); [community mirror](https://github.com/lintaojlu/submarine_cable_information)) |
| OpenStreetMap via Open Infrastructure Map | Clean | ODbL ([Open Infrastructure Map](https://openinframap.org/about)) |
| RIPE RIS Live and RIPE Atlas | Restricted | No re-packaging or redistribution; advertising-related use and commercial use of Atlas need permission ([RIPE terms](https://www.ripe.net/about-us/legal/terms-of-service/); [RIPE Atlas terms](https://www.ripe.net/about-us/legal/ripe-atlas-service-terms-and-conditions/)) |
| Cloudflare Radar | Grey area | CC BY-NC 4.0 ([Cloudflare Radar](https://developers.cloudflare.com/radar/)) |
| IODA (internet outage data) | Restricted | Every response says "All Rights Reserved" ([IODA API](https://api.ioda.inetintel.cc.gatech.edu/v2/)) |
| Internet Society Pulse | Restricted | Non-commercial only, no redistribution ([Pulse terms](https://pulse.internetsociety.org/terms/)) |

**Competitors, search and risks.** GeoTraceroute already traces routes from probes in 100+ countries onto a 3D globe and can follow real cables ([GeoTraceroute](https://geotraceroute.com/)). Cable Atlas, Hurricane Electric's 3D map and several hobby repos with zero stars fill out the field, which suggests the visual is easy to copy but hard to make spread ([Cable Atlas](https://cable-atlas.nstsdc.org/); [HE 3D map](https://he.net/3d-map/); [traceroute-globe](https://github.com/nnnnnnnnnke/traceroute-globe)). "Undersea cable" autocompletes to "map", "cut" and "cut today", so search spikes with incidents such as the Baltic and Red Sea damage, but TeleGeography and publishers dominate the head terms ([Autocomplete](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=undersea%20cable); [Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists](https://thebulletin.org/2026/02/seabed-zero-baltic-sabotage-and-the-global-risks-to-undersea-infrastructure/)). The templated pages would be one per cable (about 733), one per landing station and "Undersea cables connecting [country]", plus pages for individual cuts. India has 27 named landing points in the current data ([Submarine Cable Map](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/)). It ranks last because browsers cannot run traceroute, so you'd need server probes or reverse traceroutes. Cable choice is guesswork that must be labelled "likely". Nearly every live internet data source is non-commercial or restricted. And search pull is the weakest on the list.

### Ideas that missed the cut

**Lightning Thunder Clock** already exists: wheresthatlightning.com animates an expanding thunder wavefront to your position ([Where's That Lightning](https://wheresthatlightning.com/)). Blitzortung's terms forbid commercial projects and say outright, "Do not setup yet another visualization of online data" ([Blitzortung terms](https://www.blitzortung.org/en/contact.php)). The open satellite alternatives leave most of India uncovered, and lightningtracker.app holds most of page one with city pages ([lightningtracker.app](https://lightningtracker.app/lightning-map/new-york/new-york-city/)). **Birthday Planet** is saturated by NASA's own Hubble birthday tool ([NASA Hubble birthday](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/multimedia/what-did-hubble-see-on-your-birthday/)). **Your Stuff's Journey** has clean trade data (Harvard's Atlas is CC0) but nothing live, and "where is my phone" means Find My Device ([Harvard Dataverse](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi:10.7910/DVN/T4CHWJ); [Autocomplete](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&hl=en&gl=us&q=where%20is%20my%20phone)). **Aurora** has superb public-domain NOAA data, but My Aurora Forecast has 5M+ downloads and India almost never sees aurora, so add it as a layer rather than a product ([OVATION JSON](https://services.swpc.noaa.gov/json/ovation_aurora_latest.json); [My Aurora Forecast](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.jrustonapps.myauroraforecast)). Wind, radio, ship and air-quality globes face entrenched leaders (nullschool, Windy, Radio Garden, MarineTraffic) or restrictive sources. For example, WAQI bars paid apps and needs an agreement for for-profit use, and AISStream publishes no licence ([WAQI API](https://aqicn.org/api/); [AISStream docs](https://aisstream.io/documentation)).

## Non-commercial licences are a grey zone; provider terms are a wall

The three-way split you asked for holds up, but the line between grey and restricted matters more than it looks. **Clean** sources allow commercial use outright. That covers US government data (USGS, NOAA, NASA imagery), CC BY 4.0 (EMSC, GMN, ECMWF), CC BY-SA (HYG, Wikipedia), ODbL (adsb.lol, OpenStreetMap) and Launch Library 2, which says "you are free to use the data in any way, shape, or form" ([TSD FAQ](https://github.com/TheSpaceDevs/Tutorials/blob/main/faqs/faq_TSD.md)). **Grey area** sources use Creative Commons NonCommercial: the World Atlas, Cloudflare Radar, Global Fishing Watch and possibly TeleGeography ([GFW licence](https://globalfishingwatch.org/our-apis/documentation/docs/license-rate-limits)). **Restricted** sources set their own terms that bite regardless of how you read "NC". These include ESA's star catalogues, OpenSky, WAQI, Blitzortung, eBird, Raspberry Shake, OCEARCH ("for viewing purposes only"), RIPE, IODA and Internet Society Pulse ([OCEARCH policy](https://www.ocearch.org/data-policy/); [eBird API terms](https://www.birds.cornell.edu/home/ebird-api-terms-of-use/)).

On ads and NC, your position is reasonable but legally unsettled. Creative Commons defines NonCommercial as "not primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage or monetary compensation" and says the test "turns on the use, not the identity of the reuser". Its official interpretation page does not address advertising ([Creative Commons NonCommercial interpretation](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/NonCommercial_interpretation)). A 2008 blog post reports Creative Commons staff saying ads next to NC content are acceptable, but Creative Commons itself has not confirmed this ([Blogoscoped, 2008](https://blogoscoped.com/archive/2008-02-07-n77.html)). Movebank goes as far as calling the NC clause "vaguely defined" and recommending against it ([Movebank data policy](https://www.movebank.org/cms/movebank-content/data-policy)). The steelman case against you is that a site built around one NC dataset, with ads, is arguably "directed towards monetary compensation". The practical answer is architectural: build the core on clean sources, keep any NC layer optional and easy to remove, and email the licensor before switching ads on. Hosting has the same trap: Vercel's free Hobby plan is "non-commercial, personal use only" and GitHub Pages is not for commercial sites ([Vercel Hobby](https://vercel.com/docs/plans/hobby); [GitHub Pages limits](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/getting-started-with-github-pages/github-pages-limits)).

## Templated pages must each carry their own live numbers

Google does not ban templated pages. It bans pages "generated for the primary purpose of manipulating Search rankings and not helping users" ([Google Search Central, March 2024](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2024/03/core-update-spam-policies)). In May 2026 it went further and warned that creating separate content "for every possible variation of how people might search", including fan-out queries, violates the scaled content abuse policy, adding that "a high quantity of pages doesn't make a website higher quality" ([Google AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)). The policy page, last updated 28 August 2026, names "using generative AI tools ... to generate many pages without adding value" as an example ([Google spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)). Google has run spam updates in March, June and August 2026, and a September 2026 spam update that began on 24 September was still listed when checked on 4 October ([Google Search Status Dashboard](https://status.search.google.com/products/rGHU1u87FJnkP6W2GwMi/history)). Agency commentary claims the August update hit pages that just swap one variable into the same text while sparing entity and location data pages, but Google has not confirmed that ([digitalapplied.com](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/programmatic-seo-after-march-2026-surviving-scaled-content-ban)). The safe rules for every idea above follow from this. Use one template per real-world entity (a country, an airport, a quake, a star), and make sure each page carries numbers that genuinely differ. Never write a separate page for each way of phrasing the same question. Never mass-produce AI introductions. Noindex combinations whose data barely differs.

AI summaries make this sharper. Ahrefs found the top result's click-through rate was **58 percent lower** when an AI Overview appeared (December 2025 data). Pew found people clicked a link 8 percent of the time with an AI summary against 15 percent without one, and that summaries appeared on 60 percent of searches using question words ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overviews-reduce-clicks-update/); [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/)). Science had the highest AI Overview rate of any category in Semrush's data, at about 26 percent ([Semrush](https://www.semrush.com/blog/semrush-ai-overviews-study/)). No primary study shows that live tool pages hold up better. Orbital Radar holding first place with a live count through 2026 is circumstantial evidence that live answers still earn clicks, and pages that AI summaries cite earn about 120 percent more clicks per impression than uncited ones ([Search Engine Journal on Seer](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/ai-overview-ctr-fell-61-but-clicks-didnt-collapse/572993/)). For links, Google lists "keyword-rich, hidden, or low-quality links embedded in widgets" as link spam. So any embeddable widget should carry a plain brand link, ideally nofollow ([Google spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)). Per-event pages that journalists can cite are the durable route. An agency reports one interactive map earning over 6,000 backlinks from more than 560 domains, though that figure is self-reported ([Fractl](https://www.frac.tl/interactive-tools-link-building/)).

## A WebGL2 globe on static hosting keeps a spike cheap

Build on **three.js with its WebGL2 renderer** (r186, MIT licence, about 190 KB gzipped core), or on **globe.gl** for speed (MIT, about 511 KB gzipped all-in, by the researcher's measurement) ([npm: three](https://registry.npmjs.org/three/latest); [globe.gl bundle](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/globe.gl@2.46.2/dist/globe.gl.min.js)). WebGL2 reaches about 96 percent of users against about 86 percent for WebGPU, and Android in-app browsers are disputed for WebGPU, so treat WebGPU as an optional extra ([caniuse WebGL2](https://github.com/Fyrd/caniuse/blob/main/features-json/webgl2.json); [caniuse WebGPU](https://github.com/Fyrd/caniuse/blob/main/features-json/webgpu.json)). Avoid tools metered per view. On Mapbox, a viral month of one million map loads would cost about $3,050 by its published tiers, and Cesium ion's free plan is non-commercial ([Mapbox pricing](https://www.mapbox.com/pricing); [Cesium ion pricing](https://cesium.com/platform/cesium-ion/pricing/)). For tens of thousands of moving points, use one draw call and animate positions on the GPU from a single time value. Cap pixel density on phones and use additive glow sprites rather than full-screen bloom effects. Build a stress test on a mid-range Android phone early, because no published benchmark exists ([deck.gl performance guide](https://deck.gl/docs/developer-guide/performance)).

Host on **Cloudflare**, where static asset requests are "free and unlimited". Have one scheduled job poll every upstream source within its rules (CelesTrak every two hours, JPL never from the browser, adsb.lol at a polite rate) and write compact JSON snapshots that the CDN serves to everyone. Your upstream load then depends on the schedule, not the crowd ([Cloudflare Workers pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/pricing/)). Netlify's free plan pauses sites when credits run out, and Vercel Hobby pauses features for 30 days when you exceed its limits ([Netlify credits](https://docs.netlify.com/manage/accounts-and-billing/billing/billing-for-credit-based-plans/how-credits-work/); [Vercel Hobby](https://vercel.com/docs/plans/hobby)). Start from an approximate location based on IP at the edge, using Cloudflare's `request.cf` fields, and offer precise GPS only behind a deliberate tap ([Cloudflare request docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/runtime-apis/request/)).

Sharing needs three paths. First, server-rendered 1200x630 Open Graph cards made with Satori and cached forever per unique result. Keep them under about 300 to 600 KB for WhatsApp ([Vercel OG docs](https://vercel.com/docs/og-image-generation); [Meta WhatsApp link previews](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/link-previews/)). Second, a downloadable PNG, because Instagram captions show no link previews ([Share-preview](https://share-preview.com/blog/instagram-link-preview)). Third, a 9:16 MP4 clip encoded in the browser with WebCodecs and Mediabunny, falling back to MediaRecorder ([Mediabunny](https://mediabunny.dev/guide/introduction); [Chrome 126 release notes](https://developer.chrome.com/release-notes/126)). Android WebView, which runs Instagram's and Facebook's in-app browsers there, does not support `navigator.share`, so add an "open in browser" nudge ([MDN BCD Navigator](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/Navigator.json)). Since X began suppressing links in 2023, the media itself has to carry the brand, with the URL visible in the frame ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/10/05/x-cuts-headlines-from-link-previews-as-musk-wants-users-posting-directly-on-the-platform)).

Several facts changed recently and should be rechecked before you build. CelesTrak ran out of five-digit catalogue numbers on 11 July 2026, so new objects only exist in the newer OMM format, not the old TLE format ([CelesTrak](https://celestrak.org/)). NOAA's old solar-wind JSON paths now return 404 ([SWPC plasma URL](https://services.swpc.noaa.gov/products/solar-wind/plasma-7-day.json)). The American Meteor Society and the International Meteor Organization are still recovering from a cyberattack reported in September 2026 ([The Record](https://therecord.media/international-meteor-organization-cyberattack)). ESA now labels Gaia and Hipparcos as non-commercial. ECMWF went fully CC BY 4.0 in October 2025 ([ECMWF](https://www.ecmwf.int/en/about/media-centre/news/2025/ecmwf-achieve-fully-open-data-status-2025)). OpenSky and airplanes.live have tightened access, adsb.lol plans an API key, and Google's September 2026 spam update may still be rolling out.

## Conclusion

The opportunities cluster where a common question has an answer that keeps changing and no live page answers it. Planes is the cleanest example the research found, which is why a cheap count page is the right first experiment even before the harder encounter engine. India splits the list neatly. Ideas built on satellite or global sensor data (VIIRS night lights, USGS and EMSC quakes, star catalogues) serve Indian users as fully as American ones. Ideas built on ground volunteer networks (ADS-B receivers, lightning stations, meteor witnesses) arrive thin over India and the surrounding oceans. Because your audience is global but you are probably based in India, ideas 2, 3 and 4 are where you can tell stories, with Indian history, Indian districts and quakes felt in Indian cities, that Western competitors won't bother to tell.

Two build choices compound. Starlight Time Machine and Lost Stars share one sky engine, and planes, quakes and fireballs share one globe-and-snapshot pipeline, so the second idea on each engine costs a fraction of the first. Licensing is a design decision, not paperwork: if every core layer is clean and every NC layer can be switched off, adding ads later is a settings change rather than a rebuild. Treat every search score here as a hypothesis until a keyword tool confirms volumes. And remember that the viral moment, if it comes, will most likely arrive with a news event you cannot schedule, so the per-event pages need to be live and fast before the news breaks.
